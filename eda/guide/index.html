EDA primer

How a chip gets laid out

A digital chip begins as a description of behaviour and ends as polygons on a few dozen mask layers. Everything in between is one long, staged negotiation between what the designer asked for and what physics will allow. This is a tour of those stages: what each one is actually trying to achieve, which number it is minimising, what it is not allowed to break, and the characteristic way each one fails.

The interactive demo runs four of these stages for real, on a small design, in your browser. The rest are explained here because a flow described only as far as someone got round to implementing it is a misleading picture of the flow.

Why it is a pipeline at all

Stated in full, the problem is this: given a few hundred thousand logic cells and the connections between them, choose a position for every cell and a path for every wire, such that every signal arrives within its clock period, no wire violates any of a few thousand manufacturing rules, the whole thing fits in as little silicon as possible, and it does all of that across the full spread of temperature, supply voltage and manufacturing variation the part will be sold into.

Nobody solves that. Each piece of it in isolation is already NP-hard, the pieces interact, and the instances are enormous. So the industry does the only tractable thing: it decomposes. Each stage fixes some of the degrees of freedom, optimises what it can still move, and hands a smaller problem to the next one. Synthesis fixes what the gates are. Floorplanning fixes how much room there is. Placement fixes where the gates sit. Routing fixes where the wires go.

Decomposition is what makes the flow possible and it is also the source of nearly every difficulty in it. A stage that has fixed something cannot un-fix it, and a stage cannot see the consequences of its own decisions, because those live downstream. Placement minimises an estimate of wirelength, since the wires do not exist yet. Synthesis meets timing against an estimate of wire delay, since it has no geometry at all. Every stage is optimising a proxy, and each proxy is a little wrong.

The consequence: the flow is a loop

Drawn as a line it looks like a conveyor belt. In practice the arrows point backwards more often than anyone would like. Routing discovers that a region is too congested to wire, and the fix is not in routing — it is a placement that spread that region out, or a floorplan that gave it more area. Timing analysis after routing finds a path that closed on estimates and does not close on extracted parasitics, and the fix is a resynthesis of that path. A real project spends far more time going round this loop than it does making the first pass forward.

Synthesis

not run in the demo

Synthesis compiles register-transfer-level code — Verilog or VHDL describing registers and the logic between them — into a netlist of real cells from a real library. It happens in three broad moves. First elaboration, which turns the language into generic boolean logic and generic registers. Then technology-independent optimisation, which restructures that logic: propagating constants, sharing common subexpressions, collapsing and re-decomposing boolean networks, deciding that two adders in mutually exclusive branches can be one adder and a multiplexer. Finally technology mapping, which covers the optimised boolean network with cells that actually exist, choosing drive strengths as it goes.

The output is the thing this app takes as input, and it is worth being clear about the difference in character. Synthesis is a logic problem — a covering and restructuring problem on a graph of boolean functions. Everything after it is a geometry problem. They share almost no machinery.

Technology mapping is classically posed as DAG covering: express the optimised logic as a network of simple primitives, then cover it with patterns corresponding to library cells, minimising a cost that is area, or delay, or some blend. Cell libraries carry dozens of functions at half a dozen drive strengths each, so the choice at every node is wide, and the cost of a cell depends on what it drives — a strong gate is fast but presents a big load to whatever drives it. Sizing and buffering are therefore intertwined with mapping rather than done after it.

The reason synthesis timing is only an estimate is that there is no layout yet. Delay through a cell depends on its output load, and load is mostly wire capacitance, and wire capacitance depends on where the cells ended up. Classical flows plug this hole with wire load models, statistical guesses keyed on fanout and block size, which are notoriously optimistic. Modern flows close the gap by doing a rough placement inside synthesis — physical synthesis — precisely so the estimate is made against real distances.

The failure mode is worth stating plainly, because it is the most common expensive mistake in the whole flow. Constraints are how you tell synthesis what to care about, and it will do exactly what you say. Under-constrain a design and it optimises for area, produces something small and elegant, and misses its clock period by a factor you cannot recover downstream. Over-constrain it and it believes you: it will throw carry-select adders and enormous parallel structures at a path that had all the time in the world, and hand you a netlist twice the size it needed to be.

Why the demo skips it. Every preset in the app is a gate-level netlist built by hand in code — the ripple-carry adder instantiates XOR, AND and OR cells per bit slice and threads the carry through, the counter instantiates flip-flops and an enable chain. That is the same form synthesis would have produced, and it is exactly what a placer expects to be handed. Building a real synthesiser would mean an RTL parser, an elaborator, a boolean optimiser and a mapper, none of which would teach you anything about placement.

The netlist

runs in the demo

Not a stage so much as the handoff. The netlist is a graph: instances of library cells, and nets tying their pins together. Each net has exactly one driver and some number of loads, and reading it in means resolving all of that and rejecting the things no later stage can recover from — a net nobody drives, or a net two cells drive at once.

It is worth pausing on how little this graph contains, because it explains a great deal about how placement behaves. There is no information here about what the circuit computes. A placer handed a multiplier and a placer handed a random graph with the same degree distribution will do the same thing to both. Connectivity is the entire input.

Going deeper

The number the demo reports here that matters later is fanout — how many inputs a single output drives. A cell has a finite drive strength, and a net with more load than its driver can pull becomes a slow edge, which shows up as a timing violation two or three stages downstream and is fixed by inserting buffers. Very high fanout nets, reset and enable and above all the clock, are special-cased precisely because no single cell can drive them: they get trees of their own.

In the demo the format is one cell per line — TYPE instance PIN=net — with boundary pins declared by input and output. Boundary pins are what make the placement problem well posed: they are fixed on the die edge, and they are the only fixed points the placer has. Without them, minimising wirelength would collapse the design to a single point and be right to.

Floorplanning

runs in the demo

Floorplanning decides how much silicon the design gets, what shape it is, and how it is organised before a single logic cell is positioned. Standard cells are all the same height by construction, so the core is cut into rows of that height and every cell will eventually sit in one, its origin landing on a site — the quantum of horizontal position. Primary inputs and outputs are pinned around the edge.

On a real block there is more to it, and it is the part that takes judgement rather than computation. Large hard macros — SRAMs, PLLs, analogue blocks — are placed by hand or with help, because a badly placed memory ruins everything around it and no later stage can move it. The power grid is planned here: straps of wide metal that will carry current across the block, which consume routing resource that routing then does not get. Blockages are declared over regions where cells or wires are not allowed.

Going deeper

The arithmetic is simple: core area is total cell area divided by target utilisation, shaped to the requested aspect ratio, then rounded outward to whole rows and whole sites. That rounding is not cosmetic. Keeping the core origin on the grid by construction means "is this cell on the grid" stays a modulo test everywhere else in the flow, rather than a floating-point comparison with a tolerance.

Total area fitting is a necessary condition and not a sufficient one, which is a mistake the demo made and had to have fixed. A cell occupies whole sites in one row and cannot be split across two, so the real question is whether the set of cell widths can be packed into the available rows at all. Adding up areas can say yes while the packing says no, and the symptom appears in legalisation as a cell with nowhere legal to go. The floorplanner now runs a best-fit-decreasing packing check and widens the core until a packing exists, then reports the utilisation it actually achieved rather than the one you asked for.

Utilisation is the single most consequential number anyone sets in this flow, and the demo puts it on a slider for exactly that reason. Set it low and the placer has all the room in the world, wirelength comes out short, routing is trivial, and you have paid for silicon you are not using. Set it high and you get the opposite in every respect, with the sting that the bill arrives late: a design at 90% places perfectly well and then turns out to be unroutable, two stages and a lot of runtime later.

Global placement

runs in the demo

This is the stage worth watching, and the demo's speed control exists for it. Placement has to choose a position for every cell such that connected cells end up near each other — short wires are fast wires and cheap wires — while not piling every cell into the same corner of the core. Those two goals are in direct opposition, and the entire stage is the story of their tug of war.

The intuition for the modern approach is mechanical. Treat every net as a spring pulling its cells together, and solve for the arrangement in which all the springs are as relaxed as they can collectively be. Left alone that collapses the design onto a point, because zero wirelength really is the optimum, so add a second force: a density field over the core that pushes cells out of crowded regions into empty ones. Turn that second force up gradually. Wirelength gets worse as the design spreads, deliberately, and the placement you end up looking at is where the two forces balance.

When this stage finishes, cells are at arbitrary real-valued coordinates and may still overlap each other. That is expected. It was solving a relaxed problem on purpose.

Why HPWL. The objective is half-perimeter wirelength: for each net, take the bounding box of its pins and add half its perimeter. It is a stand-in for routed length, and a good one — it is exactly the optimal route length for two- and three-pin nets, which are the great majority, and it costs almost nothing to evaluate. Actual routed length is unknowable at this point and computing Steiner trees for every net at every iteration is out of the question.

The net model. A net with k pins is not k springs to a centroid; that would penalise spread rather than approximate a bounding box. The demo uses a bound-to-bound model: within each net, every pin is sprung to the two pins at the extremes of the net's bounding box, with weight inversely proportional to the distance between them. That weighting is what makes the quadratic objective approximate HPWL, and because it depends on where the cells currently are, it has to be rebuilt as they move. Hence sweeps: solve, re-weight, solve again.

Combining the two forces. The obvious approach — solve for wirelength, then slide cells down the density gradient — does not work, and the reason is instructive. The solve jumps straight to the quadratic optimum, so the next one undoes whatever spreading the gradient step just achieved, and the placement oscillates without converging. What works instead is putting the spreading force inside the solve, as an additional term on the right-hand side, so wirelength and density are balanced in one linear system rather than fighting in alternation. That is the force-directed formulation, and it is what the demo implements.

Why the force accumulates. Each iteration's spreading increment has to stay small, under about one bin. Scale it up to spread faster and the requested displacement lands outside the core, every cell clamps to the boundary, and the design piles up along the edges instead of spreading — which is exactly what happened when the demo's placer was tuned too aggressively. The way to reach a large total force from small increments is to accumulate them across iterations, with decay, which is stable because the density gradient reverses sign once a cell has moved past the crowding it was escaping.

Knowing when to stop. Three conditions, and getting them right took more effort than the algorithm did. Stop when overflow reaches its target and wirelength has gone flat — flat, not merely non-improving, because wirelength rises during the spreading phase and stopping the first time it fails to improve cuts the stage off early. Failing that, stop when a whole window of iterations has passed without meaningfully improving the best overflow seen, because not every design can reach the target: bins hold only a handful of cells and cell widths vary six-fold, so there is a granularity floor on how evenly area can be spread.

Why the density target is a ceiling. Overflow is measured against a maximum bin density, not against the design's average utilisation. Aiming at the average would ask a block using 40% of its core to spread until it filled every corner, and that is the wrong answer — a sparse block should sit in a compact cluster and keep its wires short. Legalisation is perfectly happy with the cluster, because the rows underneath it are only 85% full.

What a production placer does that this one does not. Two things, mainly. It is timing-driven: nets on critical paths get higher weights, so the placer shortens the wires that matter rather than treating all wires as equal. And it is congestion-aware: it estimates routing demand while placing and avoids arrangements that will be unroutable, rather than leaving that discovery to the router.

Legalisation

runs in the demo

Global placement handed over an arrangement that is good but not manufacturable: cells sit at arbitrary coordinates and overlap each other. Legalisation makes it real. Every cell moves into a row, its origin lands on a site, and no two cells occupy the same space — while moving each cell as little as possible, because the global placer's answer was the good one and every micron of displacement spends some of its quality.

The demo implements Abacus. Cells are processed in order of increasing x; each is trial-fitted into its preferred row and a band of neighbouring rows, and goes wherever total displacement grows least. Cells pushed up against each other form a cluster that is then positioned as a single unit, and clusters that collide are merged and re-solved.

Expect wirelength to get slightly worse here, and read that number carefully: a small positive delta is the sign of a good global placement, because it means the answer was very nearly legal already.

Processing cells in x order is what gives Abacus its quality, and it is also what can strand the last cell. Each row is left with a gap too narrow for anything, and a wide cell arriving late finds every row short even though the free area, added up, is ample. The demo handles this by retrying the whole assignment widest-cell-first, which places the cells with the fewest options while the choice still exists. Quality is slightly worse than the x-ordered pass, since a row is picked before its neighbours are known, so it is only used when the first pass fails.

One detail that is easy to get wrong: cluster positions are real numbers and have to land on the site grid. Because every cell width and every row origin is a whole number of sites, snapping the start of a cluster puts every cell in it on-grid too — but two abutting clusters can round towards each other and overlap by a single site, so the snapping has to carry a cursor.

Real flows follow legalisation with detail placement, which the demo does not do. Having established legality, detail placement improves it in place: reordering cells within a row window, mirroring cells about their vertical axis so that pins that need to be near each other are, redistributing whitespace so that later buffer insertion has somewhere to go. It is local search over a legal solution rather than a global optimisation, and it is where a few more percent of wirelength is recovered.

Clock tree synthesis

not run in the demo

Up to this point the clock has been a lie: one net connected to every flip-flop in the design, with a fanout no real cell could drive. Clock tree synthesis replaces it with a tree of buffers and inverters, inserted and placed so that the delay from the clock source to each of thousands of endpoints is as nearly equal as it can be made.

The reason skew matters is arithmetic. A synchronous path has one clock period to get from one flop to the next, and if the receiving flop is clocked slightly later than the launching one, that difference is taken out of the budget of that path. Skew is therefore not a clock problem, it is a tax levied on every path in the design, and a sloppy clock tree makes every other stage's job harder.

Goes wrong when

Going deeper

Its position in the flow is not arbitrary. It runs after placement because delay depends on distance and there is no way to balance a tree whose endpoints have no positions. It runs before routing because the clock is the most timing-critical net in the design and gets first claim on routing resources, often on dedicated thick metal layers with lower resistance. And it is the last stage to insert a significant number of new cells, which is the real reason placement is not allowed to fill the core: CTS needs somewhere to put its buffers.

Two families of approach. Geometric methods build a regular structure — the H-tree being the canonical one, recursively bisecting the area so that every path from root to leaf is the same length by construction. Elegant, and it assumes endpoints are spread evenly, which real designs do not do. Clustering methods instead group nearby endpoints, drive each group with a buffer, and recurse upwards, matching delay by inserting wire and buffers where needed. Production tools are closer to the second.

Beyond skew there are two numbers to know. Insertion delay is the absolute latency from source to endpoint: a deep tree with good skew still delays the whole clock, which hurts the interfaces to everything outside the block. And clock power is substantial — the clock is the one net guaranteed to switch every single cycle, and its tree is routinely a large fraction of a chip's dynamic power, which is why clock gating, shutting off subtrees whose logic is idle, is standard practice.

Two refinements worth knowing about. Useful skew deliberately breaks the balance: if one path is struggling for setup and its neighbour has slack, clocking the launching flop a little early and the receiving flop a little late moves time from where it is spare to where it is needed. And on-chip variation means two buffers built identically at opposite corners of a block do not behave identically, so signoff derates the tree — the analysis assumes the launching path is slow and the capturing path is fast, or the reverse, whichever is worse.

Global routing

not run in the demo

Routing is split into two stages because doing it in one is intractable. Global routing is the planning half: divide the core into a coarse grid of tiles, and decide for each net which tiles it passes through and roughly which metal layers it uses. No actual wire geometry is produced. What is produced is a plan that the detail router can be confident is achievable.

The quantity that governs everything here is capacity. Each tile boundary can be crossed only so many times per layer, because a layer has a finite number of tracks running across it, and the plan is only valid if no boundary is asked to carry more nets than it has tracks for. Congestion is the measure of how badly that is being violated, and relieving it is what the stage spends its time on.

Goes wrong when

Going deeper

Metal layers alternate preferred direction — one horizontal, the next vertical, and so on up the stack — because that is what makes crossings possible at all. Two nets that need to cross do so on different layers, joined to their own layer by vias. Vias are not free: each one adds resistance and consumes area on both layers, so via count is part of the objective and not an afterthought.

The standard technique is rip-up and reroute. Route every net by the cheapest path, find the tiles that are over capacity, tear out the nets crossing them and route them again with the cost of those tiles raised. Repeat. Nets that were routed early and greedily are forced to yield to nets that have no alternative, and the cost function does the negotiating — the same idea FPGA routers use under the name negotiated congestion. It converges in practice, and when it does not converge, that is information: the design as placed cannot be wired.

Which makes global routing the stage that finally tells you whether the floorplan was honest. This is where a design that placed beautifully at high utilisation reveals that it has nowhere to put its wires, and the remedy is upstream every time: more area, or a placement that considered congestion while it was still free to move things. Congestion maps out of global routing are fed back into placement for exactly this reason.

Detail routing

not run in the demo

Detail routing turns the plan into metal: specific tracks, specific segments, specific vias, for every net in the design. This is the point at which a layout becomes something that can actually be manufactured, and by a wide margin it is the most computationally expensive stage in the flow — on a large block it can run for days.

What makes it hard is not path-finding, it is the rules. Design rules are numerous, they are non-local, and they are absolute: minimum spacing between shapes, minimum area for a shape, how far metal must extend past a via, how long a run may be before its end must be treated specially, and at advanced nodes rules whose applicability depends on what is nearby for some distance. Satisfying one commonly violates another, which is why the last few violations in a congested corner can take as long as the first ninety-nine per cent of the design.

Going deeper

Everything before this stage was a model. Detail routing produces the first geometry from which resistance and capacitance can actually be extracted, and those extracted parasitics are what timing is finally signed off against. It is entirely routine for a design that looked closed on placement estimates to open back up here, because a net that the estimate said was short turned out to detour around congestion, or to climb to a thin upper layer, or to collect more vias than anyone budgeted for.

At advanced nodes the stage acquires constraints that have nothing to do with electricity. When a layer is printed with multiple exposures, the shapes on it must be two-colourable such that same- colour shapes stay far enough apart — a graph colouring problem layered on top of the routing problem. Antenna rules limit how much bare metal may be connected to a gate during manufacturing, before the rest of the circuit exists to bleed off accumulated charge, and are fixed by adding diodes or by cutting a long wire and rejoining it on another layer. And yield preferences pull towards redundancy: a doubled via where there is room for one is free insurance against a single via failing.

Static timing analysis

not run in the demo

Static timing analysis is how anyone knows whether a chip will run at its target frequency. It computes when every signal arrives at every pin, compares that against when it was required, and reports the difference. Static means it never simulates: it makes no assumption whatsoever about what data the circuit will see, which is what makes it exhaustive over inputs in a way that simulation can never be, and why it is the basis of signoff rather than a supplement to it.

Note what it does not do: STA optimises nothing. It is the measuring instrument. What it produces is slack — required time minus arrival time — and negative slack is a violation. Fixing violations is the job of the other stages, in response to what STA reports.

In
The netlist, the timing constraints, and delays from estimates or extraction
Out
Slack on every path; worst and total negative slack as headlines
Optimises
Nothing. It measures; the other stages respond
Constrained by
Setup and hold, in every process, voltage and temperature corner
Goes wrong when
A setup fix slows a path and opens a hold violation elsewhere
Going deeper

Two checks, pulling in opposite directions. Setup requires data to arrive early enough: launched from one flop, through the logic, it must be stable at the next flop's input some setup time before the clock edge that captures it. The budget is one clock period, minus that setup time, minus whatever clock skew works against you. Hold requires the data to arrive late enough: it must not race through the logic so fast that it overwrites the receiving flop's input before that flop has captured the previous value. Setup is fixed by making paths faster, hold by making them slower, and the same edit can easily fix one while breaking the other.

Setup is a frequency problem — a chip that fails setup runs slower than intended but runs. Hold is a functional problem, independent of clock frequency, and a chip that fails hold does not work at any speed. That asymmetry is why hold violations are taken so seriously so late.

Then multiply the whole analysis by the operating conditions. Transistors are slower hot and at low voltage, faster cold and at high voltage, and manufacturing spreads them either side of nominal. Each combination is a corner, and a design must hold in all of them — with the twist that setup is typically worst in the slow corner and hold in the fast one, so the two checks are not even fighting in the same conditions. Multi-mode multi-corner analysis crosses that with the design's operating modes, and the number of analyses to run gets large quickly.

The most important practical point is that STA is not a stage at the end. It runs throughout: after synthesis on wire-load estimates, after placement on distance-based estimates, after CTS with a real clock tree, and after routing on extracted parasitics, each pass more trustworthy than the last. Timing closure — the loop of measuring, making a targeted engineering change, and measuring again — is where most of the schedule on a real chip actually goes.

Signoff

not run in the demo

Signoff is the set of checks that must all pass before a design is committed to a mask set, which is an expensive and irreversible thing to be wrong about. Parasitic extraction turns the routed geometry into resistances and capacitances for final timing. Design rule checking verifies the layout against the foundry's rules, and layout-versus-schematic verifies that the layout implements the netlist it claims to — that the polygons are the circuit.

Alongside those, the physical-effects checks. IR drop analysis asks whether the power grid can actually deliver current everywhere without the local supply sagging enough to slow the logic down. Electromigration asks whether any wire is carrying enough current density to erode itself over the product's lifetime. Power analysis produces the numbers the system designers were promised. Each of these can send you back into the flow, which is why none of them are left to the end if they can be estimated earlier.

What the demo actually runs

Four stages, with real algorithms rather than animations of algorithms: the netlist compiler, the floorplanner with its packing feasibility check, an analytic force-directed global placer, and an Abacus legaliser. The placer and the legaliser are the genuine article, scaled down — the same objective, the same net model, the same failure modes, on hundreds of cells instead of hundreds of thousands. It runs in a Web Worker so the numerical loop does not freeze the page, and draws to a canvas so that thousands of flylines stay smooth.

The speed control is there because the interesting part of global placement is not the final number, it is the shape of the trade. Slow it down and watch wirelength climb while density overflow falls: that is the density force winning ground it needs, at a price in wirelength that is being paid on purpose. Pause it and step an iteration at a time if you want to see the mechanism rather than the result.
